## An elevator pitch does not close anything

Picture the setup George opens with: you get upgraded, and the prospect who would change your year is in the next seat. You have until they reach for their headphones.

A pitch has exactly one job in that moment, and it is not signing anyone. It is delivering enough value that the other person wants to keep talking. Walk in needing a signature and you have already lost the room.

Six parts build one that works. Watch George assemble them, then take them one at a time below.

## Build it in six parts

Each part earns the next. Open each to see what it looks like in practice.

:::tip Two numbers worth remembering
- **75 to 140 words** is the realistic budget for a 30 to 60 second pitch. Draft against it.
- **Three floors** is the test. If your value proposition does not land in that ride, keep cutting.
:::

## Reframe a pain they already have

The best pitches do not introduce a new problem. They reframe one the prospect is already living with.

Years ago a seller pitched a newspaper organization built entirely on print advertising, with no reason of their own to change. They did not open with "you need digital marketing." They opened with what the room already cared about:

:::info The Fort Worth pitch
"You have sold a lot of advertising. I have too. And one thing we both know is that the advertising you sell is coming under attack. The client's first comment on the next call is always 'it isn't working like it used to.' It's not your ad that's the problem. It's that they've got broken listings, bad reviews, and a website that takes forever to load. What if the thing you sell could work better, because their virtual doorway finally matches the ad that sent someone looking for it?"
:::

Nobody was asked to abandon what they were good at. The pitch named a pain the room already felt, advertising getting blamed for problems it did not cause, and pointed at a fix that made their existing product stronger. Five years later the sales leader who was in that room brought it up unprompted, which is the real test of whether a pitch stuck.

If you sell more than one thing, you need more than one pitch: one per audience, one per problem you solve.

## Why some pitches stick

George closes by recommending a book on exactly this question. It is worth naming precisely, because two well-known books have near-identical titles.

| Book | Author | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Made to Stick** | Chip and Dan Heath | Why some ideas survive and others die. This is the one referenced in the video. |
| Make It Stick | Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel | The science of successful learning, a different subject entirely. |

*Made to Stick* argues memorable ideas tend to share six traits: simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and told as a story. Read your own pitch against that list and the weak spot is usually obvious.

:::tip Try it now
Pick your next prospect. Write your pitch in four lines: the goal, the value in one sentence, your edge, and the question. Say it out loud. If it takes longer than three floors, cut it and say it again.
:::

## Where this leaves you

A pitch is not a speech you finally got right. It is six parts, cut short, aimed at a pain the prospect already feels, and rehearsed on real people until it holds up.

Do that and you get the meeting. What you do with the meeting is the next thing to get right.
