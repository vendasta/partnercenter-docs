## A presentation is not a recap

A summary of everything you found is an audit, and nobody signs an audit. A presentation points forward: here is the future state we are taking you to, here is what we will hold ourselves to, and here is the strategy that gets you there.

It also has to work on people who are not in the room. You might present to a decisive executive while a detail-oriented finance lead reads the deck afterwards, so the format has to serve both. And it is a conversation throughout, not a speech you deliver and then stop.

Five stages do that, always in the same order. Watch George lay them out, then take them one at a time.

## The five stages

Each stage earns the right to make the next one. Open each to see what it looks like in practice.

<Accordion
  items={[
    {
      id: 'insight',
      title: 'Open with insights, not with what you sell',
      badge: '1',
      content: (
        <>
          <p>Lead with what you learned: partly industry knowledge the prospect may not have themselves, partly the specific things they told you during discovery. George calls it digging for nuggets. If a landscaping owner said half her quote requests never get followed up, that line opens the meeting, not your company history.</p>
          <ul>
            <li>Keep talk about your own company short. It is the least interesting part of the room to them.</li>
            <li>Spend real time on context before you show anything you sell.</li>
          </ul>
        </>
      )
    },
    {
      id: 'strategy',
      title: 'Give the strategy at ten thousand feet',
      badge: '2',
      content: (
        <>
          <p>Describe what you will deliver and how it connects back to those insights, without dropping into which button does what. Outcomes, not configuration: "automated follow-up on every quote request within an hour" rather than "a CRM automation with a conditional trigger on lead status."</p>
          <ul>
            <li>Open with their highest-value use case rather than building up to it.</li>
            <li>Keep the deck portion tight. Longer time on slides correlates with worse outcomes, not better ones.</li>
          </ul>
        </>
      )
    },
    {
      id: 'performance',
      title: 'Put measurement on the table early',
      badge: '3',
      content: (
        <>
          <p>Commit up front to how you will prove it is working, rather than leaving reporting to the end or, worse, to ninety days after they buy. Everyone is thinking about ROI and everyone is a little skeptical of salespeople, so volunteering the scorecard is what separates you.</p>
          <ul>
            <li>Ask the buyer to name the financial impact they want, then build the metrics from their figures.</li>
            <li>Agree the measures before building any report, then set the cadence for reviewing them.</li>
          </ul>
        </>
      )
    },
    {
      id: 'tactics-ask',
      title: 'Then the tactics, and the ask',
      badge: '4',
      content: (
        <>
          <p>Now the features, benefits, and what it costs. Go only as deep as the prospect leads you, and move the rest to the appendix. This is also where the investment slide lives, which makes it the moment to ask for the business, out loud, in the room: <em>"That's the plan. Can we get you started this week?"</em></p>
          <ul>
            <li>Talk about business value more than product features, which is what separates strong reps from average ones.</li>
            <li>Raise price once value is established, rather than opening on it.</li>
          </ul>
        </>
      )
    },
    {
      id: 'appendix',
      title: 'Park the rest in the appendix',
      badge: '5',
      content: (
        <>
          <p>Everything the room did not need but someone else might: detailed specifications, terms, contract sections, and the capability you suspect matters but are not sure about. Name it rather than hiding it: <em>"There's a webchat feature that might fit too. There's a page on it at the back, but I didn't want to build our conversation around something you haven't seen."</em></p>
          <ul>
            <li>Build two versions: a visual deck for the room, and one that reads on its own without you.</li>
            <li>Curate rather than dump. The appendix serves the personas who were not in the meeting.</li>
          </ul>
        </>
      )
    }
  ]}
/>

:::tip Try it now
Write one sentence for each stage for your next prospect: the insight you heard, the strategy, how you will prove it worked, the ask, and what goes in the appendix. Say the ask out loud beforehand. If it sounds like a question about whether you should be asking, rewrite it.
:::
