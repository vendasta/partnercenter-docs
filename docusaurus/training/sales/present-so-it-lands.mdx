## A presentation is not a recap

A summary of everything you found is an audit, and nobody signs an audit. A presentation points forward: here is the future state we are taking you to, here is what we will hold ourselves to, and here is the strategy that gets you there.

It also has to work on people who are not in the room. You might be presenting to a decisive executive while a detail-oriented finance lead reads the deck afterwards, so the format has to serve both.

Five stages do that, always in the same order. Watch George lay them out, then take them one at a time.

:::tip Naming the appendix out loud
"There's a webchat feature that might be a fit for you too. I've put a page on it at the back in case you want to look at it, but I didn't want to build our whole conversation around something you haven't seen yet."
:::

## The insights come from the discovery call

Stage one is only as good as the conversation before it. Three questions do most of the work, and the order matters.

| Ask this | Why it works |
|---|---|
| "What's on your mind?" | Opens with their agenda instead of yours, and tells you what they think the problem is. |
| "And what else?" | The second and third answers are usually the honest ones. Keep asking until they run out. |
| "So what's the real challenge here for you?" | Moves from symptoms to the thing actually blocking them, in their words. |

Two habits make those questions land. Come in with a **hypothesis, not a script**, so you can be wrong out loud and get corrected. And when something genuinely does not fit, **say so** — telling a prospect that one piece is not right for them buys more credibility than a clean sweep ever does.

Then recommend, in that order: understand, map, recommend. A recommendation that arrives before the understanding is just a pitch you did not earn, and prospects can tell the difference.

## Present to the room you actually have

The same content lands differently depending on who is listening.

- **Decisive, assertive buyers** want the numbers and the point. Skip the testimonials, the personal opinions, and the flowery language.
- **Detail-oriented buyers**, such as a finance lead, want the workflow specifics and the methodology. Give them enough to verify, and reference the appendix for the rest.
- **Executives** get the outcome summary. **Practitioners** get how it actually works day to day.

## Make it a conversation, not a monologue

George is blunt about this: the presentation is not you pontificating. It is you making a point and then getting a reaction, the whole way through.

- Successful demos average 21% more speaker switches per minute, and keep any single monologue near 76 seconds.
- Prepare questions rather than talking points, with planned pauses that ask how this fits their process.

:::tip Try it now
Write one sentence for each stage for your next prospect: the insight you heard, the strategy, how you will prove it worked, the ask, and what goes in the appendix. Say the ask out loud beforehand. If it sounds like a question about whether you should be asking, rewrite it.
:::

## Keep the promises you just made

A presentation creates commitments: the report you offered, the follow-up you scheduled, the thing you said you would check. Those are what the prospect remembers.

## Where this leaves you

A presentation that closes is not a better-looking deck. It is insights they recognise, a strategy at the right altitude, measurement offered before it is demanded, and an ask made out loud.

Do all that and you will still hear "I need to think about it." That is not a no, and it is the last thing left to learn.
