## Managing is a different job, not a promotion for selling well

Organisations tend to hand management to whoever sold the most, which quietly assumes the two jobs are the same. They are not. The best rep on the team is measured on their own numbers; a manager is measured on everyone else's.

George has held every rung from team lead to chief revenue officer, and he opens on the part most new managers get wrong: your job is developing the talent already on your team, every day, not out-selling them.

Thirteen tactics follow. Watch them, then work through each one below.

## Define pipeline stages you can actually verify

Tactic 7 is the one most worth doing this quarter, because a pipeline built on confidence percentages tells you nothing. A rep can set 60% to keep you happy. Replace the percentage with something you can check.

| Stage | Not this | This |
|---|---|---|
| Qualified | "Feels like a fit" | Budget, authority and timeline confirmed by the buyer |
| Discovery done | "We had a good call" | A written problem statement the buyer has agreed with |
| Proposal | "Sent the deck" | Presentation delivered to the actual decision-maker |
| Committed | "90% sure" | Redlined contract returned, or a start date agreed |

Build these with your top contributors rather than for them, so they own the definitions. And expect more than one pipeline: an SDR booking needs-analysis calls is not running an account executive's enterprise process, however much simpler one shared pipeline would look.

Watch for the red herring George names: deals pushed through stages they never really cleared, which show up as churn six months after signing.

## Turn a target into a plan

George's two analogies carry this one. Trying to lose weight by standing on the scale every morning does not work, because the scale is the lag measure; what you eat and what you burn are the lead measures, and they are the only things you can act on today. And wanting to see the ocean in Vancouver stays a wish until there is a date, a route, and a fuel budget.

Applied to a quarter, that is four moves:

1. **One or two goals**, each phrased "from X to Y by when". Not five.
2. **Lead measures** the team can move weekly, each sitting next to the lag measure it drives.
3. **A scoreboard** somebody looks at without being asked to.
4. **A review cadence** where seeing the number actually changes what you do next.

## What the one-on-one actually looks like

George makes the link himself: master the line-by-line and you master the one-on-one. His own example is a board member running it on him across a hundred accounts while he was unprepared. The point is not the ambush, it is that a rep who cannot talk through their own book does not yet know it.

| What you ask | What the answer tells you |
|---|---|
| "Talk me through your top ten accounts." | Whether they know their book or only their spreadsheet |
| "What are you working on there, and why does it matter to us?" | Whether they understand the deal or just the logo |
| "When did you last speak, and when next?" | Whether something is quietly drifting |
| "Where are you stuck?" | The thing you are actually in the room to help with |

Hold it weekly where you can: Gallup finds managers who have a meaningful conversation with each report every week see better engagement, performance and retention. And close the loop, because one-on-ones and floor time both backfire when what you hear goes nowhere.

## One book worth naming precisely

George credits the trust and safety material to Simon Sinek's *Start with Why*. It is worth pointing at the right title, because Sinek wrote two well-known books and the safety one is the other.

| Book | What it actually covers |
|---|---|
| **Leaders Eat Last** | The Circle of Safety: why teams that feel protected from internal threat pull together. This is the trust and safety material behind tactic 2. |
| Start with Why | Purpose and the golden circle: why an organisation exists, and how that inspires people. |

Both are worth your time. If you are working on tactic 2 specifically, start with *Leaders Eat Last*.

## Where the reps' habits meet yours

Several tactics here are the manager-side mirror of the core path. Reading them together is worth the few minutes.

| Your rep is learning | Your job as their manager |
|---|---|
| The line-by-line on their top accounts | Running the line-by-line with them, and noticing what they cannot answer |
| Leading versus lag measures on their own numbers | Turning the knobs at team level, and pairing each lag measure with a lead one |
| The 60/30/10 split of their day | Protecting the 60%, and making the 30% recurring rather than annual |
| Handling the five objections | Spotting which objection the whole team keeps losing to, and coaching that one |

## Try it this week

:::tip Try it now
Pick one rep and do a real line-by-line: their top ten accounts, no spreadsheet. Note where they went blank, because that is your coaching plan, not theirs. Then look at your own calendar and find where the admin sits. If it overlaps the hours your team is on the phone, move it.
:::

## Where this leaves you

Managing a sales team is not selling at a higher volume. It is developing people, making it safe to bring you bad news early, defining a pipeline that means something, and measuring honestly enough to act mid-quarter.

Thirteen tactics is a lot to hold at once. Pick the two your team would feel this month and start there.
