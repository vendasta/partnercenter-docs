## An objection is information, not a no

An objection almost never means what it sounds like on the surface. *"I don't know you"* means "reassure me." *"It's not my decision"* means "I do not want to decide alone." *"I need to think about it"* means "I have doubts I have not said out loud."

Every one of them is a request for information, not a locked door. The reps who close more are not the ones who avoid objections. They are the ones with a process for working through them.

Two short videos cover this. The first names the five objections you will hear most and what each one is really telling you.

## The five objections you will hear most

Almost every push-back is one of five. Naming which one you are facing tells you what to say back.

## Work the sequence before you answer

Neither video names an order of operations, so here is one worth borrowing. LAIR runs before you reach for any specific response, and the sequence matters more than any single line inside it.

| Step | What it means | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **L** — Listen | Let them finish, uninterrupted. Do not jump to a rebuttal. | On the phone, "mm-hmm" or "I see" rather than dead silence. |
| **A** — Acknowledge | Tell them the concern is valid, then probe for what sits under it. | "It sounds like you're worried about X. Can you tell me more?" |
| **I** — Identify | Repeat the concern back in your own words and confirm you have it right. | "So it's the contract length, not the price itself?" |
| **R** — Redirect | Respond with a question first, then the answer, then the proof. | "Can I bounce a few thoughts off you?" |

## Listen longer than feels comfortable

The instinct when an objection lands is to fill the silence. The data says do the opposite.

- Top sellers pause roughly five times longer after an objection than average reps do.
- The best reps hold around 43% of the talk time. Average reps talk into the high sixties.

:::tip Two numbers worth remembering
- **74%** of all objections fall into just five recurring types, which is why naming the type is most of the work.
- **40 to 60%** of qualified deals are lost to no decision at all, not to a competitor. Indecision is the real rival.
:::

## The close is the start, not the finish

You get through the objections, they say yes, and the instinct is to move on to the next deal. That is where accounts quietly leak.

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| 30 days | Prove what changed. Bring one number that moved, however small. |
| 60 days | Keep asking the discovery questions, now that you do not have to. |
| 90 days | Turn their result into their story, and ask for the referral. |

Two things to watch for after that. A **one-degree drift**, where nothing is wrong but the relationship is slowly cooling, is far cheaper to correct than a save. And **at-risk is not the same as lost**: a client saying they are unhappy is still talking to you, which is a better position than silence.

When you do lose one, lose it well. Ask what would have had to be true, thank them, and leave the door open. Some of the best re-signs are accounts that left and came back.

Take an objection you heard this week. Name which of the five it was, then run it through LAIR out loud. Now pick one bounce-back technique you did not use in the moment and rewrite your response with it.
:::

## Where this leaves you

An objection is the prospect telling you where the gap is. Name which of the five it is, run LAIR before you answer, and pick the technique that fits the moment.

That completes the series: a day that produces conversations, a full funnel, a pitch that earns the meeting, a presentation that closes, and a process for the push-back. The skill check is where you find out what stuck.
